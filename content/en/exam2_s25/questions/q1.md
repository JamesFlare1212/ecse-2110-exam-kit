## Question

Why the field winding coils in a synchronous machine are usually connected in series.

## Guide

Relate the current in a series circuit to the ampere-turns of the rotor's field coils. Distinguish equal current magnitude from the alternating magnetic polarity established by the coil connections.

## Part a

Why the field winding coils in a synchronous machine are usually connected in series.

### Solution

Use Kirchhoff's current law for a series circuit and the magnetic-circuit relation between coil current and magnetomotive force. [Lecture 10, pages 4](/pages/lecture-10/4.webp) and [7](/pages/lecture-10/7.webp), supplies the field-excitation and magnetic-flux context; the series-current conclusion follows from circuit continuity:

$$
I_{f,1}=I_{f,2}=\cdots=I_f,\qquad \mathcal F_k=N_k I_{f,k}
$$

The question asks why the rotor field coils are connected in series. With equal turns $N_k=N$ and comparable pole magnetic paths, substitute their common current into each pole's ampere-turns:

$$
\mathcal F_1=NI_f,\quad \mathcal F_2=NI_f,\quad \Phi_k\approx\frac{NI_f}{\mathcal R_k}
$$

Thus similar pole reluctances $\mathcal R_k$ give similar flux magnitudes. The winding sense and connections alternate north and south poles; equal current does not mean that every pole has the same polarity. A series connection prevents unequal branch resistances from dividing the excitation current unevenly. It ensures equal current even when coil resistances differ, although unequal turns, reluctance or saturation can still produce unequal flux.

For identical pole coils and magnetic paths, the resulting symmetry is

$$
\boxed{I_{f,k}=I_f\ \Longrightarrow\ |\mathcal F_k|=NI_f\ \Longrightarrow\ |\Phi_1|\approx|\Phi_2|\approx\cdots}
$$
