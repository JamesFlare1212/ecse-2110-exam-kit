## Question

What is synchronous speed and why synchronous machines can operate at synchronous speed only?

## Guide

Count electrical cycles per mechanical revolution using the number of pole pairs. Then ask what relative motion between the rotor and the rotating stator field would do to their load angle in steady operation.

## Part a

What is synchronous speed and why synchronous machines can operate at synchronous speed only?

### Solution

Use the formula sheet's [frequency–speed relation](formula:synchronous-speed), also shown in [Lecture 10, PDF page 9](/pages/lecture-10/9.webp). A $P$-pole winding has $P/2$ pole pairs, so one mechanical revolution corresponds to $P/2$ electrical cycles:

$$
f=\frac{P}{2}\frac{n_s}{60},\qquad n_s=\frac{120f}{P}\ \mathrm{rpm}
$$

Here $f$ is the balanced three-phase supply frequency in hertz and $P$ is the total pole count. Synchronous speed is the mechanical speed of the fundamental rotating stator magnetic field, not a rotor winding's electrical frequency. The question provides no numerical $f$ or $P$, so the answer remains symbolic.

For a synchronous motor, define $\delta$ as the electrical angle by which the stator field leads the rotor field. If their mechanical speeds are $n_s$ and $n_r$ in revolutions per minute, then

$$
\frac{d\delta}{dt}=\frac{P}{2}\frac{2\pi}{60}(n_s-n_r)
$$

In a steady synchronous state, magnetic locking maintains a constant load angle, so its derivative vanishes. Substitute $d\delta/dt=0$; a persistent speed difference would instead make the angle drift and destroy the fixed torque-producing alignment. Consequently,

$$
\boxed{n_r=n_s=\frac{120f}{P}\ \mathrm{rpm},\qquad \frac{d\delta}{dt}=0\quad\text{in steady synchronous operation}}
$$
