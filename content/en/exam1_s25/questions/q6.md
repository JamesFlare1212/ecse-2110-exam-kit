## Question

In the 3-phase circuit shown below, the line voltage magnitude is 173 kV. The phase voltages are: $E_{an}=100\angle90^\circ$, $E_{bn}=100\angle-30^\circ$, and $E_{cn}=100\angle210^\circ$. Load impedances are $Z_a=5+j0$ ohm, $Z_b=0-j5$ ohm and $Z_c=3+j4$ ohm.

## Guide

The source neutral $n$ and load neutral $o$ are disconnected. Apply Kirchhoff's current law at the floating load neutral rather than assuming its potential is zero. Keep the printed capacitive $Z_b=-j5\,\Omega$ and use kilovolts for the 100-unit phase voltages, so division by ohms yields kiloamperes.

## Part a

Calculate the neutral voltage $E_{on}$.

### Solution

Use the floating-neutral node equation of Lecture 3, pages 24–25. Define $E_{on}=V_o-V_n$ and currents from each source terminal toward the load neutral:

$$
\sum_{k=a,b,c}I_k=0,\qquad I_k=\frac{E_{kn}-E_{on}}{Z_k},\qquad
E_{on}=\frac{E_{an}/Z_a+E_{bn}/Z_b+E_{cn}/Z_c}{1/Z_a+1/Z_b+1/Z_c}
$$

The printed phase phasors are 100 kV rms: the line magnitude is $\sqrt3(100)=173.205\,\mathrm{kV}$, rounded to 173 kV in the question. Retain the specified 100 rather than rescaling it. Convert the voltages and admittances to rectangular form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{an}&=j100\,\mathrm{kV},\quad E_{bn}=86.6025-j50\,\mathrm{kV},\quad E_{cn}=-86.6025-j50\,\mathrm{kV},\\
Y_a&=0.2\,\mathrm S,\quad Y_b=\frac1{-j5}=j0.2\,\mathrm S,\quad
Y_c=\frac{3-j4}{3^2+4^2}=0.12-j0.16\,\mathrm S,\\
Y_a+Y_b+Y_c&=0.32+j0.04\,\mathrm S.
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiply each actual voltage by its admittance. The numerator has units kA, and division by siemens restores kV:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{an}Y_a&=j20\,\mathrm{kA},\\
E_{bn}Y_b&=(86.6025-j50)(j0.2)=10+j17.3205\,\mathrm{kA},\\
E_{cn}Y_c&=(-86.6025-j50)(0.12-j0.16)=-18.3923+j7.8564\,\mathrm{kA},\\
E_{on}&=\frac{-8.3923+j45.1769}{0.32+j0.04}\,\mathrm{kV}\\
&=\frac{(-8.3923+j45.1769)(0.32-j0.04)}{0.32^2+0.04^2}\,\mathrm{kV}\\
&=\frac{-0.878461+j14.792305}{0.104}\,\mathrm{kV}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Use the second quadrant for the polar angle, since the real part is negative and imaginary part positive:

$$
E_{on}=\boxed{(-8.44674+j142.23370)\,\mathrm{kV}=142.4843\angle93.3986^\circ\,\mathrm{kV}}
$$

### Source correction

PDF page 13 prints $0+j5$ in the node equation, although the question and capacitor diagram give $Z_b=0-j5\,\Omega$. The printed numerical answer actually corresponds to the negative sign. The answer's “142.5 … V” loses the kilo prefix: 100 kV divided by ohms produces kA, and the neutral voltage is in kV. Lecture 3, page 25 repeats these sign/unit inconsistencies; it is cited for the node method, not as independent numerical verification. The 173 kV line magnitude is rounded from the explicitly specified 100 kV phase magnitudes.

## Part b

Calculate $I_a$, $I_b$ and $I_c$.

### Solution

Use Ohm's law for the load phase voltages, with the same current directions as part (a):

$$
I_a=\frac{E_{an}-E_{on}}{Z_a},\qquad I_b=\frac{E_{bn}-E_{on}}{Z_b},\qquad I_c=\frac{E_{cn}-E_{on}}{Z_c}
$$

From part (a), $E_{on}=-8.44674+j142.23370\,\mathrm{kV}$. Substitute it with the printed impedances; kV divided by $\Omega$ gives kA:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_a&=\frac{j100-(-8.44674+j142.23370)}5
=\frac{8.44674-j42.23370}5
=1.689348-j8.446740\,\mathrm{kA},\\
I_b&=\frac{86.60254-j50-(-8.44674+j142.23370)}{-j5}
=\frac{95.04928-j192.23370}{-j5}\\
&=(95.04928-j192.23370)(j/5)=38.446740+j19.009856\,\mathrm{kA},\\
I_c&=\frac{-86.60254-j50-(-8.44674+j142.23370)}{3+j4}
=\frac{(-78.15580-j192.23370)(3-j4)}{25}\\
&=\frac{-1003.40220-j264.07790}{25}=-40.136088-j10.563116\,\mathrm{kA}.
\end{aligned}
$$

As a check, their rectangular sum is $(1.689348+38.446740-40.136088)+j(-8.446740+19.009856-10.563116)=0\,\mathrm{kA}$. Converting to magnitude and quadrant-correct angle gives

$$
\boxed{I_a=8.61402\angle-78.6901^\circ\,\mathrm{kA},\quad I_b=42.88970\angle26.3099^\circ\,\mathrm{kA},\quad I_c=41.50283\angle-165.2551^\circ\,\mathrm{kA}}
$$

### Source correction

The source omits explicit units on the final current list. With the printed kV source and ohm loads, those numerical magnitudes are kiloamperes. Reading them as amperes would silently replace the stated source by a 173 V supply.
