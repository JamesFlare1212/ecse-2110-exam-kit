## Question

Why do transformers in operation produce a humming noise?

## Guide

Connect the alternating voltage to the changing flux in the magnetic core. Then distinguish the electromagnetic operation from the mechanical motion that makes sound. Lecture 4, PDF page 25, supplies the flux–voltage relation; the official answer on solution PDF page 3 identifies magnetostriction.

## Part a

Why do transformers in operation produce a humming noise?

### Solution

Use Faraday’s winding relation from Lecture 4, PDF page 25, with voltage polarity chosen along the winding’s induced voltage:

$$
v(t)=N\frac{d\Phi(t)}{dt},\qquad B(t)=\frac{\Phi(t)}{A_c}
$$

The question asks for the origin of sound during AC operation. For a sinusoidal supply, integrating the voltage gives alternating core flux. The official solution identifies magnetostriction: the ferromagnetic core changes its dimensions as its magnetization changes. Its strain is approximately unchanged when the flux reverses sign. A simple qualitative model, with an unspecified material constant $k$, makes that even symmetry explicit:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(t)&=B_m\cos\omega t,\\
\epsilon_m(t)&\approx kB^2(t)=kB_m^2\cos^2\omega t\\
&=\frac{kB_m^2}{2}\bigl(1+\cos2\omega t\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the core repeatedly expands and contracts, vibrating the laminations and surrounding structure and radiating sound into the air. Winding forces and loose parts may add noise; magnetostriction is the principal explanation sought here. The quadratic model explains the common dominant twice-supply-frequency component, rather than claiming an exact strain law for every core:

$$
\boxed{\text{Alternating core magnetization}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{magnetostrictive vibration}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{audible hum},\qquad f_{\mathrm{hum}}\approx2f.}
$$
