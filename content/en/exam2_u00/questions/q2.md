## Question

Single choice questions. 3 points each.

## Guide

Distinguish supply frequency, spatial phase sequence and rotor excitation. Use synchronous speed for frequency changes, the induced-voltage relation for excitation changes, and ideal-transformer ratings for the autotransformer. In the last item, add the three spatial winding fields using the stated identical current phases; distinguish a fundamental field from possible spatial harmonics.

## Part 2-1

Which of the following can change the rotation speed of a synchronous motor:

A. Altering the frequency of voltage applied to stator winding.

B. Altering the current magnitude applied to rotor winding.

C. The sequence of appearance of the three-phase windings on the stator

### Solution

Use the formula sheet's [synchronous-speed relation](formula:synchronous-speed), also shown in [Lecture 10, PDF page 9](/pages/lecture-10/9.webp), with $p$ the number of poles:

$$
n_s=\frac{120f}{p}
$$

For the same motor, $p$ is fixed. Changing frequency from $f$ to $f'$ gives $n_s'/n_s=f'/f$. Rotor current changes excitation, while reversing spatial phase sequence changes rotation direction rather than the speed magnitude. Thus the speed-changing control is

$$
\boxed{\text{A: change the stator supply frequency.}}
$$

## Part 2-2

Which of the following can change the rotation direction of a synchronous motor:

A. Altering the frequency of voltage applied to stator winding.

B. Altering the current magnitude applied to rotor winding.

C. The sequence of appearance of the three-phase windings on the stator.

### Solution

Use the rotating-field construction in [Lecture 10, PDF page 8](/pages/lecture-10/8.webp). A balanced positive-sequence current set in spatially separated windings produces a field of the form

$$
\mathbf B(t)=B_0\bigl[\cos(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf x}+\sin(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf y}\bigr]
$$

Interchanging two spatial phase assignments reverses the sign of the rotating component. Changing positive frequency changes its rate, and changing rotor current changes excitation rather than this sequence. The reversed field and choice are

$$
\mathbf B_{\mathrm{rev}}(t)=B_0\bigl[\cos(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf x}-\sin(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf y}\bigr],\qquad
\boxed{\text{C: reverse the spatial phase sequence.}}
$$

## Part 2-3

Which of the following can change the magnitude of the internal generated voltage $E_A$ of a synchronous motor:

A. Altering the frequency of voltage applied to stator winding.

B. Altering the current magnitude applied to rotor winding.

C. The sequence of appearance of the three-phase windings on the stator.

### Solution

Use the formula sheet's [induced RMS-voltage relation](formula:generated-voltage), also shown in [Lecture 10, PDF page 14](/pages/lecture-10/14.webp), where $N$ is the effective series-turn count and $\Phi$ is flux per pole:

$$
|E_A|=4.44fN\Phi
$$

At fixed operating frequency, rotor current controls $\Phi$. In the unsaturated approximation, $\Phi=k_f I_f$, giving $|E_A|=4.44fNk_f I_f$. Consequently the intended excitation control is

$$
\frac{|E_A'|}{|E_A|}=\frac{I_f'}{I_f}\quad(f\text{ fixed, linear magnetic circuit}),\qquad
\boxed{\text{B under the fixed-frequency convention.}}
$$

### Source correction

The printed item does not state fixed frequency. Option A can also change $|E_A|$ because the same governing relation contains $f$, if rotor flux remains fixed and the motor remains synchronized. B is the conventional excitation-control answer, but the literal unrestricted wording does not establish a unique choice. Saturation also prevents assuming exact proportionality to field current in general.

## Part 2-4

The same synchronous motor is used in two cases. In case one, a three-phase $100\,\mathrm{V}$ $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ AC voltage is applied to its stator winding. The angular speed of its rotor is $\omega_{m1}$. The motor delivers $10\,\mathrm{hp}$ (horse power) to the load. In case two, a three-phase $100\,\mathrm{V}$ $60\,\mathrm{Hz}$ AC voltage is applied to its stator winding. The angular speed of its rotor is $\omega_{m2}$. The motor also delivers $10\,\mathrm{hp}$ (horse power) to the load.

A. $\omega_{m1}>\omega_{m2}$

B. $\omega_{m1}<\omega_{m2}$

C. $\omega_{m1}=\omega_{m2}$

### Solution

Convert the formula sheet's [synchronous-speed relation](formula:synchronous-speed), also shown in [Lecture 10, PDF page 9](/pages/lecture-10/9.webp), to mechanical radians per second:

$$
\omega_m=\frac{2\pi}{60}\frac{120f}{p}=\frac{4\pi f}{p}
$$

The pole count is unchanged. With synchronous operation in both cases, substitute the actual frequencies; equal output horsepower changes required torque rather than the synchronous speed:

$$
\frac{\omega_{m2}}{\omega_{m1}}=\frac{60}{50}=1.2,\qquad
\boxed{\omega_{m1}<\omega_{m2}\quad\text{(B).}}
$$

## Part 2-5

If we reconnect an ordinary transformer into an auto-transformer, then the rated apparent power of the autotransformer can be higher than the rated apparent power of the original transformer.

A. True.

B. False.

### Solution

Use the ideal-transformer voltage, current and apparent-power relations in [Lecture 4, PDF page 26](/pages/lecture-04/26.webp). Denote the original winding ratings by $V_1,V_2,I_1,I_2$:

$$
S_{\mathrm{two}}=V_1I_1=V_2I_2
$$

For a series-aiding autotransformer connection, let winding 1 be the common winding and winding 2 the series winding. The output voltage is $V_1+V_2$ and the series-winding current can reach its original rating $I_2$. Therefore the terminal rating, derived without increasing either winding's voltage or current rating, is

$$
S_{\mathrm{auto}}=(V_1+V_2)I_2
=\left(1+\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)S_{\mathrm{two}}>S_{\mathrm{two}},\qquad
\boxed{\text{A: True, subject to connection and insulation ratings.}}
$$

## Part 2-6

A synchronous generator provides power to a $RL$ series circuit. If we increase the angular speed of the rotor of the synchronous generator, which of the following is true?

A. The magnitude of the internal generated voltage $E_A$ will increase.

B. The frequency of the output voltage will not change.

C. The power factor of the load will increase.

### Solution

Use the formula sheet's [frequency](formula:synchronous-speed) and [generated-voltage](formula:generated-voltage) relations, also shown in [Lecture 10, PDF pages 9](/pages/lecture-10/9.webp) and [14](/pages/lecture-10/14.webp), together with the series-$RL$ impedance definition:

$$
f=\frac{pn}{120},\qquad |E_A|=4.44fN\Phi,\qquad
\mathrm{pf}=\frac{R}{\sqrt{R^2+(2\pi fL)^2}}
$$

Assume unchanged rotor field current, flux and fixed positive load values $R,L$, with no infinite bus constraining speed. Increasing $n$ increases $f$ and $|E_A|$. It also increases inductive reactance, so power factor decreases. This gives

$$
n'>n\ \Longrightarrow\ f'>f,\quad |E_A'|>|E_A|,\quad\mathrm{pf}'<\mathrm{pf},\qquad
\boxed{\text{A under fixed excitation.}}
$$

### Source correction

The printed question leaves excitation control unspecified. The stated conclusion assumes unchanged rotor flux; an automatic regulator could change it. An isolated $RL$ load is also assumed, because a generator synchronized to an infinite bus cannot independently increase its steady-state speed.

## Part 2-7

A synchronous machine has three-phase stator windings evenly placed on its stator with a sequence of $a$-$c'$-$b$-$a'$-$c$-$b'$. Now suppose we supply the windings with AC current with frequency of $60\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and magnitude of $10\,\mathrm{A}$, and the current phasors in three sets of windings have the same angle. Which of the following can be correct about the generated stator magnetic field?

A. counterclockwise rotating magnetic field with constant magnitude,

B. clockwise rotating magnetic field with constant magnitude,

C. The magnetic field has time-varying magnitude but does not rotate.

### Solution

Use spatial superposition of winding MMFs, the construction underlying [Lecture 10, PDF pages 8](/pages/lecture-10/8.webp) and [14](/pages/lecture-10/14.webp). For identical current phases, let $i(t)=I_{\mathrm{pk}}\cos(120\pi t+\alpha)$ and place the fundamental winding axes at $0$, $2\pi/3$, $4\pi/3$:

$$
F_1(\theta,t)=N_1i(t)\sum_{k=0}^{2}\cos\left(\theta-\frac{2\pi k}{3}\right)
$$

Adding the three spatial sinusoids gives $1-1/2-1/2=0$ for the cosine coefficient and $0+\sqrt3/2-\sqrt3/2=0$ for the sine coefficient. Hence the ideal sinusoidally distributed fundamental vanishes. If the actual winding contains a third spatial harmonic $N_3\cos(3\theta)$, its three contributions instead add in phase:

$$
F_1=0,\qquad
F_3(\theta,t)=3N_3I_{\mathrm{pk}}\cos(3\theta)\cos(120\pi t+\alpha)
$$

The latter has fixed spatial nodes and a time-varying amplitude; it is a pulsating standing field. The precise qualification to the available choice is

$$
\boxed{\text{C can occur with spatial harmonics; the ideal fundamental is zero.}}
$$

### Source correction

Equal current phasors are a zero-sequence set, not the usual balanced set with phase angles separated by $120^\circ$. Under the sinusoidally distributed winding model explicitly used on [Lecture 10, PDF page 14](/pages/lecture-10/14.webp), the resultant is identically zero, so none of the options describes a nonzero fundamental field. C is possible when uncancelled spatial harmonics exist, but cannot be asserted as a nonzero field from the printed data alone. Three identical currents also require independent winding supplies or a neutral/return path; a three-wire star connection would prohibit them. The unspecified RMS/peak meaning of “magnitude” does not affect this cancellation.
